My Name

Author

Joey Stanley

Published

November 9, 2026

Modified

November 9, 2026

I rarely encounter issues with people who get my name wrong. But I regularly find myself unsure about how to refer to other people (and am grateful for this list of linguists and IPA transcriptions of their names). So, following Gretchen McCulloch and a broader effort to encourage people to clarify how their name is used, here is details on my name, what to call me, and related topics.

Pronunciation

I pronounce my name, in broad IPA, as /ˈd͡ʒoʊ.i ˈstæn.li/. There should be no surpriseo here. This is what what I hear from most American English speakers.

I’m hesitant to give a narrow IPA transcription for two reasons. First, I’m not sure whether it should be [d͡ʒoʊi], [d͡ʒoʊwi], [d͡ʒoʊʷi], [d͡ʒowi], or something else. Perhaps I’m overthinking it. Second, because I raise, front, diphthongize, and nasalize /æ/ before nasals (i.e. ban-raising or the prenasal split), I’m not sure how to transcribe the /æ/ in Stanley. In my dissertation I transcribed this sequence as something like [ɛ̃ɛ̝̟̯̃].1

1 My first homework assignment in my intro to linguistics class was to transcribe my name in IPA. When I asked my professor what that vowel in Stanley was, they said [æn] and I knew for sure it wasn’t that!

Feel free to pronounce my name in the best way that it matches on to your phonology. If you don’t have prenasal-/æ/-raising, feel free to use a true [æn] rather than mapping it on to another vowel (like perhaps /ɛ/). If you speak a language that doesn’t have [d͡ʒ] and typically pronounce the letter j as [ʒ], that’s fine. However, I might not recognize it if you use a [j]—regardless of where in the post-alveolar or palatal place of articulation you use, I expect some sort of (af)frication.

Stanley

Though Stanley is an English name, coming from “stan” (“stone”) and “leah” (“woodland clearing” or “meadow”), I don’t have English ancestry. In my family, the name comes from my Polish my great-grandparents who had the name Stanilka. The story goes that when their children were enlisting in the war (I forget which one), if they were to be captured, they would be tortured less if they had an English name rather than a Polish name. So they all changed their names from Stanilka to Stanley.2 Since I know there is some variation, I do spell my name with an ey at the end.

2 My dad has an uncle I think who was already born Stanley Stanilka, so he became Stanley Stanley!

FWIW, no I don’t have a Stanley mug, no I’ve never seen Flat Stanley, no Stanley isn’t my favorite character from “The Office,” and no I’m not going to name my kid Yelnats.

I live in Utah and I know there were Stanleys that settled in various parts of Utah (Heber and Nephi, among others). I’m not related to them because my ancestry does not trace back to early Mormon pioneers and early migrants to Utah.

Joey

My legal name is Joseph, from the book of Genesis in the Old Testament. I pronounce it [d͡ʒoʊsɨf], with a voiceless fricative and a higher-than-schwa unstressed vowel. But I’m not too concerned about how you say it because 1) I know there’s lot of variation in how the name is said across languages and 2) I prefer you not call me Joseph.

I’ve always gone by Joey. I have many brothers and almost all of us used the diminutive versions of our names when we were little. They all eventually switched to the monosyllabic versions. I never felt like I grew out of Joey. So yes, it’s Joey, not Joe and not Joseph. In fact, when I see mail that is addressed to Joseph, it’s either a legal document or junkmail because everyone I’ve ever met knows that I go by Joey.

What I publish as

Until May 2026, I published under the name Joseph A. Stanley3. This goes back to the decision I made when submitting an abstract to my first conference presentation in 2012. I thought Joseph A. Stanley sounded more professional so I went with it. But I have regretted it ever since. So, as a celebration for getting tenure, in May 2026 I decided to publish as Joey Stanley going forward.

3 The A. has a period since it is an abbreviation of my middle name, Abraham. Supposedly, I was going to be Abraham Joseph Stanley (and probably would have gone by A.J.–such was the ’90s) but when my grandmothers heard about it around the time I was born, they were not impressed, so my parents switched it to Joseph Abraham.

I realize that this has some consequences: Zotero, for example, treats “Joseph A. Stanley” as a different person than “Joey Stanley”. In references sections, the two are treated differently and are therefore sorted out of order: “Stanley, Joey (2026)” will now appear before “Stanley, Joseph A. (2025)” even though many citation styles prefer the oldest paper on top. In the in-text citations, Zotero might automatically disambiguate it as it would with other authors who share a last name by doing something like “(Stanley, J. A. 2025)”. I’d prefer to have it all be treated the same, so that “Joey Stanley” and “Joseph A. Stanley” are treated as “allo-names” of the same “name-eme”(?), but I know styleguides might prohibit that.

For what it’s worth, different versions of my name have already been floating around. For example, my dissertation has my full name, Joseph Abraham Stanley.4 I also have blog posts that have been cited rightfully as “Joey Stanley” since that’s the name on my website. So, I’ve got three published names already. Might as well start making it all consistent by going by Joey everywhere.

4 I think using full names is common in dissertations and theses. I at least dutifully transfer that full name to my citations because I don’t want to make presumptions about what authors prefer.

Address forms

I’ve been concerned with forms of address since my first solo-authored conference presentation. I grew up in a religious culture where children and adolescents never called adults by first name. That continued into college and it took me until my PhD was in-hand to finally call any faculty member by first name.5 However, I recognize that language changes and first names are becoming more common, so I usually don’t really care whether people use first name or title.

5 I’ve got stories about that.

My preferences listed below are ultimately based by the professor-student relationship that may exist between us. The more prototypical, the more I prefer titles. In this table, TLN = “title + last name” and FN = “first name”.

Group Preferred address form
Traditionally-aged students in my courses TLN unless we’ve have some significant outside-classroom interaction like an RAship, TAship, mentored research, directed readings, thesis committee, numerous extended office hours interactions, or multiple (usually three) courses together
Other BYU linguistics students TLN
Linguistics undergraduates at other institutions TLN
High schoolers TLN
BYU grad students TLN at first, but one conversation is enough to switch to first name
Non-traditional students FN
My students who have graduated FN
Non-linguistics students FN
Non-BYU linguistics grad students FN
Any academic FN
The public TLN is nice the first time, but then FN after that

In general, my email signature is the clue. Whatever I sign is what I expect in return.

I have a slight preference for Doctor6 (which reflects my education) over Professor (which reflects my employment). I very much prefer either of those over Mister. In Latter-day Saint religious settings, Brother is fine and Doctor, Professor, and Mister are not. (In the past I have gone by President and Elder, depending on religious service assignments at the time.) Even though BYU is owned and operated by the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, I find it inappropriate to refer to me as Brother Stanley while I’m at work.

6 I can’t help but wonder if that low vowel in Doctor also plays a role (cf. Boberg 1999).

Other

In English, I use he/him pronouns. In other languages, I use masculine pronouns if they exist.

When discussing my religion, I generally follow this style guide. In particular, the first instance of the name of my church should be “The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints”. I use term Latter-day Saint, both as a noun and an adjective, when discussing myself and other members of my faith. I am also generally okay with LDS and Mormon (in that order).
